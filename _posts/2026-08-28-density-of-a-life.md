Every Sunday evening, I shade one square on a chart. The chart assumes that I will live to eighty in good health, and each square represents one week. About 26.5 percent of it is now black. The exercise makes time visible because once a square is filled, it cannot become blank again. Yet the chart also reveals the limits of measurement. Every past week occupies the same amount of space, but the weeks do not carry the same significance. Some hold almost nothing I can recover; others have changed the direction of everything after them. That gap between a week’s fixed size and its changing weight has become the way I understand my life. Time gives every week the same dimensions, but how I inhabit it gives it weight and thickness.

The first three years of my chart are thin because I remember almost nothing from them. The years before I turned twelve also feel distant. My world was smaller then, and fewer experiences connect those weeks to the person I am now. This does not make childhood unimportant, but it shows that the density of a life is not simply the number of days lived. A dense week creates connections that remain active after it ends. It changes how the next week can be understood. Since entering college, I have felt that density increase. Study and travel have repeatedly placed me outside what I already knew. My life gained depth because new experiences forced me to revise old assumptions. I therefore do not measure a life only by its duration because its more meaningful measure is density, the degree to which lived time expands a person’s ability to understand the world and participate in it.

My ambition has always been expansive rather than linear. When something looks worth knowing, I want to move toward it. That desire explains my decision to study both Computer Science and Global Studies. In high school, I read an article that compared society to a train. Science provides the power, while the humanities determine the direction. The comparison remains useful to me because technical capacity cannot determine the values it should serve. Computer science trains me to define a problem precisely and construct a system that works. Global Studies asks who defined the problem and how that system will operate within a social context. Engineering draws me because it gives ideas material consequences. For that reason, technical competence must be accompanied by humane judgment. Studying both fields allows each perspective to expose the limits of the other, enriching my view of the world.

Dance gives me a form of expression that written language and code cannot provide. Both writing and programming work through symbols in a two-dimensional world, but dance requires me to think and communicate through a body moving in space. When I practice hard, a handstand or a front walkover, a movement that was once impossible for me, becomes possible. Each new physical capacity expands the ways I interact with the world and express an idea without words. Dance also teaches me that every body has physical limits, but disciplined practice can shift them. This combination of constraint and creation is why dance adds to the density of my life. It gives me an embodied and artistic form of knowledge that academic study cannot reproduce.

Travel increases that density in a different way by placing my assumptions in contact with other cultures and communities. Last summer, I had planned to accept a fellowship internship creating digital media for a local nonprofit. The work interested me and would have strengthened my professional experience. At the same time, I had long wanted to study in Italy and visit the places connected to the work of Elena Ferrante, my favorite author. Her writing was the reason I had begun learning Italian three years earlier. I did not know when I would have another opportunity to connect the language and literature I had studied with the place from which they emerged. The choice was between two worthwhile uses of the same limited summer.

A conversation in my Global Futures class clarified that choice. When our class spoke with Kim Stanley Robinson, the author of *The Ministry for the Future*, someone asked what advice he had for college students. He answered, “Go watch fire, have sex, and throw rocks.” I understood his point as a warning against spending an entire life preparing to live. By then, however, the regular deadline for the study-abroad program had passed. I asked for a reference letter on short notice and submitted a late application. I was fortunate to be admitted at the last minute. In Italy, the language I had studied and the places behind Ferrante’s writing became part of a lived setting. The experience moved me beyond my usual boundaries and gave me a more grounded understanding of cultural difference.

None of these choices means that a dense life must be crowded or that every week must be extraordinary. Experience can remain superficial when it is collected but never examined. Density requires enough attention for an experience to alter how I understand and act. My two majors make different forms of knowledge answerable to each other. Dance enlarges what my body can know and express. Travel tests my assumptions against ways of life shaped by cultures different from my own. The chart can record the length of my life, but it cannot record these changes in capacity and perspective. That part remains my responsibility. I cannot keep a square blank, but before it turns black, I can decide how fully I will inhabit the time inside it.
