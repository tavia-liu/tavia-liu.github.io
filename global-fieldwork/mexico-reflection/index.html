May 2024 / Cuetzalan, Mexico

Fieldwork changed what I think research is responsible for.

This reflection comes from a May 2024 field study in Cuetzalan, Mexico on migration, extractivism, Indigenous sovereignty, community resistance, and life-affirming practices amid systemic violence.

I arrived in Mexico thinking about global citizenship as a moral vocabulary: responsibility, awareness, respect, solidarity. I left understanding it more as a discipline of attention. It is easy to say that the world is interconnected; it is harder to sit with the concrete forms of that interconnection when they appear as migration enforcement, extractive development, land dispossession, language politics, and the everyday labor of communities protecting life under pressure.

Our conversations made abstraction difficult. Migrants and organizers did not speak about policy as a distant institution. They spoke about families, routes, borders, remittances, fear, refusal, and dignity. Indigenous youth organizers and community elders did not describe sovereignty as an academic keyword. They described the work of defending territory, water, memory, and collective decision-making against projects that arrive with the language of progress while redistributing risk onto the people least protected by the state.

The most important lesson was that resistance is not only opposition. In the communities we visited, resistance also looked like cooperative organization, intergenerational teaching, food, ceremony, local governance, and careful forms of hospitality extended to strangers like us. Those practices changed how I understand research. Good research is not extraction with better language. It requires humility about what one does not know, accountability to the people whose lives become data, and enough patience to let the field unsettle the frame you brought with you.

As a computer scientist, this experience matters because AI systems increasingly mediate institutions: who is classified, who is searched, who is seen as risky, whose language is legible, whose history becomes searchable, whose claim is treated as credible. My Global Studies training keeps pulling me back to those questions. I want to build technically strong systems, but I also want to build systems that remember the political world they enter.
